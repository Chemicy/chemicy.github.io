Venezia, anno 1494

Double-Entry Bookkeeping

Every amount is written down twice. Venetian merchants worked this out more than five hundred years ago, and companies all over the world still keep their books this way.

Per Cassa // A Cavedal … ducati 1000

“Debit Cash // Credit Capital, 1,000 ducats.” This is how the first entry of a new business looked in a fifteenth-century Venetian journal. By the end of this page, you will be able to read it.

Capitolo IStart with a problem

Imagine you open a small shop and keep a notebook: one line for every amount that comes in, one line for every amount that goes out. This is a cash book, also called single-entry bookkeeping. It is how most people track their own spending.

The shop’s cash book
What happenedInOut
Opened the shop, put money in the till1,000
More money came in500
Bought stock600
Sold goods900

The notebook can tell you that the till now holds 1,800. It cannot answer these questions:

Was that 500 earned or borrowed? If it was borrowed, the shop now owes someone money. How much of the stock is left? Did the shop actually make a profit this month? If a number was copied down wrong, how would you ever notice?

A cash book only watches one thing: the till. Double-entry bookkeeping starts from a different idea. Every transaction has a source and a destination, and you record both. When money goes into the till (the destination), you also record whether it came from the owner, a lender or a sale (the source), with the same amount on each side. Kept this way, the books answer all of the questions above, and the two sides must always add up to the same total. If they don’t, something was recorded wrong.

Capitolo IIWhere it came from

The ducatA Venetian gold coin minted from 1284. Its weight and purity stayed reliable for centuries, so it became a trusted currency across Mediterranean trade. All the examples below are priced in ducats.

From the thirteenth to the fifteenth century, the cities of northern Italy were the commercial centre of Europe. Venetian ships brought pepper and silk from the East and sold them on to buyers north of the Alps. A single voyage could take months, several partners often pooled the money for it, and goods were usually sold on credit. Keeping track of who had invested what, who owed whom, and whether a voyage had made money was more than memory and a cash book could handle.

Merchants gradually settled on a method. Each trading partner and each kind of property got its own page, every page was split into two columns, and every transaction was written on two pages at once. No single person invented it; it was worked out over generations at the merchant’s counting desk. The layout with debits and credits on facing sides became known as the Venetian method (alla veneziana).

  1. 1202

    Fibonacci finishes the Liber Abaci

    Leonardo of Pisa, better known as Fibonacci, introduces Hindu-Arabic numerals to Europe. Adding up columns with the digits 0 to 9 is far easier than with Roman numerals like MCCII, and double-entry books depend on it.

  2. 1300

    The earliest double-entry books

    The Florentine firm of Farolfi already used double entry in its accounts for 1299–1300. The 1340 ledgers of the city of Genoa are the oldest surviving complete set of double-entry books.

  3. 1458

    Cotrugli’s manuscript

    Benedetto Cotrugli, a merchant from Ragusa (today’s Dubrovnik), includes a chapter on bookkeeping in his Book of the Art of Trade. It circulated only as a handwritten copy and was not printed until 1573.

  4. 1494

    Pacioli’s Summa is printed in Venice

    The friar and mathematician Luca Pacioli publishes his Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalità. One section, Particularis de computis et scripturis, sets out the Venetian merchants’ bookkeeping method in thirty-six short chapters. It is the first printed textbook on double entry.

  5. 1543

    Across the Alps

    Bookkeeping manuals based on Pacioli appear in Dutch and in English this year, and the method spreads through Europe as the centre of trade moves north. Goethe later has a character in one of his novels call it one of the finest inventions of the human mind.

  6. 1854

    Bookkeeping becomes a profession

    The Society of Accountants in Edinburgh, the first professional body of accountants, receives a royal charter. The Institute of Chartered Accountants in Ireland follows in 1888, and by the twentieth century double entry is the standard for businesses worldwide.

Why VeniceAround 1494, Venice was the printing capital of Europe; Aldus Manutius opened his famous press there in the same years. Pacioli’s book was set in movable type and could be copied by the hundreds, which is why it travelled so much further than any manuscript.

Who was Pacioli?

Luca Pacioli (c. 1447–1517) was born in Sansepolcro, a small town in Tuscany. As a young man he worked as a tutor for a merchant family in Venice and saw first-hand how merchants kept their books. He later became a Franciscan friar, taught mathematics at universities across Italy, and worked alongside Leonardo da Vinci in Milan. Leonardo drew the illustrations for another of his books.

Pacioli was clear that he was describing the method of Venice, not his own invention. Because he was the first to put it in print, he is still called the father of accounting.

Per and AIn Pacioli’s journal, each entry names the debit after Per and the credit after A, with two slashes between them. The line at the top of this page follows that format.

He told merchants to keep three books. The memorandum (Memoriale) was for jotting down events as they happened. The journal (Giornale) rewrote each event in date order as “debit this, credit that, this much”. The ledger (Quaderno) gave every item its own page, and the journal’s figures were copied onto the right pages. Modern accounting software is still built on the same two layers: a journal and a ledger.

Capitolo IIIOne equation

Every rule of double entry follows from a single equation. First, three terms.

Assets are what the shop owns: cash in the till, pepper in the warehouse, money customers owe the shop.

Liabilities are what the shop owes to others: a loan from a banker, unpaid bills from suppliers.

Owner’s equity is the part of the assets that really belongs to the owner: the money the owner put in, plus profits earned and not yet taken out.

Everything in the shop was paid for either with other people’s money or with the owner’s money. There is no third source. So:

Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equitywhat the shop has = what it owes others + what belongs to the owner

This equation holds at every moment. If a transaction adds 500 to the left side, then either the right side also grows by 500 (money was borrowed, for example), or something else on the left shrinks by 500 (cash was swapped for goods). Either way, every transaction touches at least two places. That is what “double” means.

Adding the business in motion

Once trading starts, three more things change the owner’s equity: revenue increases it, expenses reduce it, and drawings (money the owner takes out for personal use; in a company, dividends) also reduce it. Give these their own lines, move the items that reduce equity to the left side, and the equation becomes:

Assets + Expenses + Drawings = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity + Revenuethree kinds on the left, three on the right; this split is used in the next chapter

Capitolo IVDebit and credit mean left and right

Dr and CrLedgers abbreviate debit as Dr and credit as Cr, usually traced to the Latin debitor (one who owes) and creditor (one who is owed). Italian merchants wrote dare (to give) and avere (to have).

In the ledger, each item gets its own page, called an account. The page is divided down the middle, and drawn this way it looks like the letter T, so it is called a T-account.

Account name (for example, Cash)
DebitDare, the left side
CreditAvere, the right side

This is where most beginners get stuck: debit does not mean bad, and credit does not mean good. The earliest accounts were kept for people. A debit meant “this person owes me” and a credit meant “I owe this person”, so the words made literal sense. Once accounts were opened for things that aren’t people, such as cash, goods and revenue, the words kept only their position. You can safely read them as “left column” and “right column”.

There is only one rule: in every transaction, the total entered on the left must equal the total entered on the right. Textbooks put it as “every debit has an equal and opposite credit”.

That leaves one question: when an account increases, does the amount go on the left or the right? It depends on which side of the equation the account sits.

Capitolo VThe DEA-LOR mnemonic

Go back to the expanded equation from Chapter III. The three kinds of account on its left side are increased on the left (debit). The three on its right side are increased on the right (credit). Their initials spell DEA-LOR.

Other versionsSome teachers read the six letters as DEALER. In Ireland and the UK, many learn DEAD CLIC: Debits are Expenses, Assets and Drawings; Credits are Liabilities, Income and Capital. All of them say the same thing.

Using it takes three questions. Which two (or more) accounts does this transaction touch? Is each one a DEA account or a LOR account? Is it going up or down? If it goes up, record it on its own side. If it goes down, record it on the opposite side.

You may have seen a bank statement say your account was “credited” when money arrived, which seems to contradict “assets increase on the debit side”. The bank is keeping its own books. The money in your account is money the bank owes you, so to the bank it is a liability (L). A liability increase is a credit. The rule is the same; what changed is whose books they are.

What the ledger tells Marco after seven entries

How much he earned: revenue of 900 minus expenses of 500 (400 cost of goods sold, 100 rent) is a profit of 400 ducats.

What the shop has: 1,650 in cash and 200 in pepper, so 1,850 in assets.

Who it belongs to: 500 is owed to the banker. Marco’s share is his 1,000 of capital plus 400 of profit minus 50 of drawings, which is 1,350. And 500 plus 1,350 is exactly 1,850, so the equation holds.

Every question the cash book in Chapter I could not answer can now be read straight from the accounts. Adding up all the debit balances and all the credit balances to check that they match is called a trial balance. Pacioli is said to have warned merchants not to go to bed until their debits and credits agreed.

Capitolo VIIThree quick questions

For each one, ask: is this a DEA or a LOR account, and is it going up or down?

    Capitolo VIIITake these with you

    Every transaction is recorded in at least two places, and the left side always equals the right side.

    Debit means left and credit means right. Don’t read anything more into the words.

    Drawings, Expenses and Assets (DEA) increase on the debit side; Liabilities, Owner’s Equity and Revenue (LOR) increase on the credit side. Decreases go on the opposite side.

    All of it follows from one equation: Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity.

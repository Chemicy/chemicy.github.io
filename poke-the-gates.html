Computers only know two words

A computer has no mouth, and it can't read English. Inside, it has countless tiny switches, and each switch can only be in one of two states: power on, which we call 1, or power off, which we call 0. That tiny piece of information is called 1 bit.

One switch can only answer "yes" or "no." But line up 4 switches and they can show the 16 numbers from 0 to 15. Each bulb has its own value: the rightmost one is worth 1, and moving left they're worth 2, 4 and 8. Add up the bulbs that are lit, and that's the number. Try poking the bulbs:

So what is a logic gate?

A logic gate is a little box with its own rule. It looks at the 0s and 1s coming in and, following its rule, decides whether a 0 or a 1 goes out.

There are only a handful of common rules, but snap them together and you get an entire computer. Let's start with the silliest level of all: no gate whatsoever.

LV.0Nickname: The Honest Wire

No GateWIRE

1 goes in, 1 comes out. 0 goes in, 0 comes out. Nobody in the middle is up to anything. Remember it as: says what it hears

No gate? That's right, this level really has no gate! Flip a light switch and the light turns on; flip it again and it turns off. The wire is empty in between, with nobody messing with the signal.

It sounds almost too simple, but it builds the most important intuition: the switch is the signal, the light is the result. Get this honest version firmly in mind, and later, when a gate pulls a trick, you'll spot it right away by comparison.

LV.1Nickname: The Contrarian

NOT gate

Give it a 1 and it insists on 0. Give it a 0 and it insists on 1. Remember it as: flip it

LV.2Nickname: The Both-or-Nothing Gate

AND gate

It outputs 1 only when both inputs are 1. If even one of them is 0, the output is 0. Remember it as: needs both

LV.3Nickname: The Any-One-Will-Do Gate

OR gate

If at least one input is 1, it outputs 1. Only when both are 0 does it output 0. Remember it as: any one will do

LV.4Nickname: The Odd-One-Out Gate

XOR gate

XOR stands for "exclusive OR": one or the other, but not both. When the two inputs are different (one 0, one 1), it outputs 1. When they're the same (both 0 or both 1), it outputs 0. Remember it as: only when different

LV.5Gates team up

How does a computer do 1 + 1?

In a world with only 0 and 1, 1 + 1 equals 10 (read "one-zero," which is 2 in everyday decimal). You write 0 in the ones place and carry 1 to the next place.

Look closely: the ones place is 1 only when the two numbers are different. Isn't that exactly XOR? And there's a carry only when both numbers are 1. Isn't that exactly AND? Put the two side by side, and a circuit that can add is born. It's called a half adder.

Stamped!

Chain lots of these adding circuits together, each one catching the carry passed along by the one before, and you can add big numbers like 238 + 947. The instant you press "=" on a calculator, a whole row of these circuits flickers at once.

A half adder can only handle one column. But in real addition, each column has to add A and B plus the carry sent over from the column on its right. Adding three things at once needs a stronger circuit: the full adder.

Inside, a full adder is really just two half adders and an OR gate. Line up 4 full adders, each passing its carry to the left, and you get a tiny calculator that can do 15 + 15. It works exactly like column addition by hand, except instead of carrying when you reach ten, you carry when you reach two.

What's the point? What does this have to do with my life?

The little gates you poked today are all around you, making billions of decisions for you every day. First, let's see how they go from a grain of sand all the way to the phone in your hand. Poke each step of the staircase:

QWhere are logic gates? Why have I never seen one?

They're hidden in the chips inside phones, computers, game consoles, TVs, washing machines and elevators, so small you can barely see them under a microscope. A high-end phone chip has tens of billions of transistors, and a few transistors together make one logic gate.

QWhy do computers only use 0 and 1?

Because "power on / power off" is the hardest thing to get wrong. It's like shouting across a noisy playground: yelling just "yes" or "no" is much harder to mishear than reading out a long string of numbers. A computer makes billions of decisions every second, so reliability matters most.

QWhat are they doing while I play games?

Checking things at lightning speed, every single frame: jump button pressed AND character standing on the ground, so jump. Touching an enemy AND not invincible, so lose a life. Rhythm games work the same way: you press the key AND the note is exactly on the line. Perfect!

QI'm not going to build chips. Is this still useful?

Yes. Outlining an essay, breaking a math problem into cases, deciding with friends where to go on the weekend: all of these mean splitting a big problem into small "yes / no" pieces, then connecting them with "and," "or" and "not." Once your thinking is clear, the problem gets simple.

QWill I use this if I learn to code?

Every day. Almost every programming language has them; in Python they're literally called and, or and not. Here's the go-outside decider from above, written in Python:

if raining and not umbrella:
    print("Run!")

QWho came up with all this?

In 1854, the English mathematician George Boole turned "and," "or" and "not" into mathematics, now called Boolean algebra. In 1937, a young man named Claude Shannon realized that switches in an electrical circuit could do Boolean algebra. Today's computers grew from there. Fun fact: Claude, the AI that helped build this page, is named after Mr. Shannon.

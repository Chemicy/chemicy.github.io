🎻→🎹 Chords for Violinists

You play one string at a time. Why learn chords?

Because you've been playing with them all along. Nobody sent you the memo.

A violinist's world is horizontal: one note after another, lined up into a melody. A pianist's world is vertical: one hand comes down and several notes ring at once. A chord is that vertical thing: three or more different notes sounding together. (Two notes make an interval, which is a chord's building block.)

First, hear the difference. The keys up top light up as they play

The same four notes. Horizontal is melody, vertical is a chord. That's all there is to it, and music schools can spend four years on it.

Claudia is at the keyboard up top. Every ▶ button in this lesson hands me a score: I play it, the keys light up, and the little note under the keyboard tells you exactly how I played it and which notes I used. You just listen.

The keyboard covers only two octaves, on purpose: the leftmost key is exactly your open G string, and the four keys with orange dots are your four open strings, G, D, A and E. The whole keyboard is roughly the territory you can reach without shifting position, just laid out flat.

Three chord things you've secretly done already

  • Tuning. When you bow two open strings together and listen until they hum as one instead of fighting, you're checking a perfect fifth by ear.
  • Double stops. Two strings at once is already half a chord. The kind where you crunch three or four strings at once is officially called a chord on the page.
  • Arpeggios. After scales come arpeggios, do-mi-sol-do up and down. That's a chord taken apart and played one piece at a time. Your teacher just never told you its real identity.
📜 Word archaeology: "chord" is a historical typo

In Middle English it was spelled cord, short for accord (agreement, harmony). Accord comes from Latin ad (toward) plus cor (heart): literally, hearts pointing the same way. So a chord originally meant several notes reaching an agreement.

Around 1600, educated writers decided cord looked a lot like the Greek chordē (gut string, as in the string of an instrument), and helpfully added an h. And so an agreement between notes ended up spelled exactly like the string on your violin. Strings really were made of twisted sheep gut back then. A pure typo, but a rather romantic one.

The Chinese word for chord, 和弦 (héxián), literally means "harmonizing strings." And harmony comes from the Greek harmonia, a carpentry term for a joint: fitting pieces together without a gap.

How this course goes

First we'll visit the lab and watch two of the purest sound waves go on a date, to see why some combinations sound sweet and others clash. Then we'll discover that a chord is already hiding inside a single string. Next we'll watch humanity argue for two thousand years about how to tune instruments. Only then do we get to the actual chord recipes and decode the alphabet soup. At the end there's a dojo where you can practice as much as you like. Nobody's grading.

The 440 Lab: two waves on a blind date

Sound is air shaking. How many times it shakes per second is the frequency, measured in Hz.

🔤 Alphabet soup: Hz

Pronounced hertz. Named after the German physicist Heinrich Hertz, the first person to prove experimentally that electromagnetic waves exist. He died at 36, but tuners all over the world say his name every day.

Who is 440 Hz? It's your open A string, the A the oboe plays for everyone to tune to before a concert. An international conference in London made it the standard pitch in 1939, and in 1955 the ISO (International Organization for Standardization) adopted it as ISO 16. Before that, every place tuned its own way: 415 was common in the Baroque era, some church organs went above 460, and traveling musicians were thoroughly miserable.

Real instruments produce messy mixtures of sound, so the lab uses sine waves: the purest possible sound, containing just one frequency. It sounds like the beep from a hearing test.

📜 Word archaeology: sine is a string too

Ancient Indian mathematicians called it jyā, meaning bowstring. Arab scholars transliterated it as jiba, and later Latin translators misread it as a different Arabic word, jaib (a fold of a garment, a bay), and translated that into Latin as sinus. Three hand-offs later, the word still couldn't escape the string. The Chinese term, 正弦 (zhèngxián), happens to keep the character for string.

Why do doubling and halving blend so well?

Every time 880 shakes twice, 440 shakes exactly once, so every cycle lines up perfectly. They line up so well that your brain can't be bothered to treat them as two notes and simply reports one note, just brighter. Halving to 220 works the same way, just darker.

This is consonant to the point of boring: no friction at all, so no drama. When men and women sing the same line together, they're a factor of two apart in frequency, yet everyone says they're singing the same note. To human ears, two notes a factor of two apart are the same character in a different outfit, which is why they share a name: both are called A.

📜 Word archaeology: octave

Latin octavus, eighth. Count from do up to the high do: do re mi fa sol la si do, 8 notes including both ends. Notice that the ancients counted the starting point too; they had no concept of starting from zero. This fencepost way of counting will trip you up again later, so hold a grudge against it now.

Why do some combinations clash? Two kinds of noise

  • Beats. When two frequencies differ by just a little, they push together for a moment and then fight each other, so the volume swells and fades: wah, wah, wah. The number of wahs per second is exactly the difference between the two frequencies. This is what you listen for when you tune: the wahs get slower and slower, then disappear, and you're in tune.
  • Roughness. Once the difference goes past twenty or thirty hertz, the wahs come too fast to count and blur into a chainsaw-like buzz. The cause is inside your cochlea: the two frequencies are so close that they tug on the same small patch of sensory cells, and those cells can't tell which one to listen to.

The person who wrote all this down was the 19th-century German polymath Hermann von Helmholtz, in his 1863 book On the Sensations of Tone. Along the way he also invented the ophthalmoscope. He was the kind of colleague who kept other scientists up at night.

Here's the pattern

The simpler the ratio between two frequencies (2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 5:4), the more often the waves realign, and the more harmonious they sound. The messier the ratio, the longer they take to realign, and the more awkward they sound. This is first principle number one of chords: pleasant-sounding notes played together have frequencies in simple whole-number ratios.

⚠️ An honest confession from the lab

Pure sine waves are too clean. A combination like the tritone, whose ratio is far from any simple fraction but whose two frequencies are fairly far apart, actually sounds only odd, not harsh. Every note on a real instrument comes with its own string of overtones (next chapter), and it's the overtones beating against each other that make things truly harsh. Don't believe it? A chord has been living inside one string all along

When you play your open G string, do you think all that comes out is 196 Hz? Strings are greedy: while the whole string vibrates, it also splits into 2, 3, 4 segments that each vibrate on their own, all at the same time. These extra vibrations are called overtones, and their frequencies are exactly 2, 3, 4, 5 times the lowest note (the fundamental), and so on up the line.

You've met them. The natural harmonics you play by lightly touching the string at 1/2, 1/3 or 1/4 of its length are just one of these overtones invited out to sing solo.

🎻 This is your G string. Tap a number to bring out that overtone on its own

Tap a number above. 👆 marks where you'd lightly touch the string.

Overtones #4, #5 and #6 are G, B and D, in the ratio 4:5:6. Pull them out and play them on their own, and you get a G major chord.

This is first principle number two of chords, and the most important one: the major chord wasn't invented by anyone. It was copied from the inner structure of a single note. Take what's already inside one string, spread it out across a few separate notes, and you get a chord. It sounds stable and natural because your ears respond: isn't this just one note, viewed under a magnifying glass?

🔤 Alphabet soup and word origins

Fundamental: Latin fundamentum, foundation. Overtone: translated from Helmholtz's German Oberton (upper tone). Harmonic: also means overtone, and shares a root with harmony, that carpentry joint. The three words get used interchangeably, so don't panic.

Monochord: mono (one) plus chord (string). A wooden box with a single string stretched over it and a movable bridge. Legend says Pythagoras used it to measure the ratios 2:1, 3:2 and 4:3. One-string players studying chords? You have a patron saint.

🎻 A bonus just for violinists

The B of overtone #5 is about 14 cents lower than the piano's B (cents are explained in the next chapter). Overtone #7 is even stranger: about 31 cents below the nearest F on the piano. The piano doesn't have this note at all, though blues musicians love it. Remember this oddity, because it's the spark that started the two-thousand-year dispute in the next chapter.

Equal temperament: a two-thousand-year fight over slicing the cake

If simple fractions sound best, why not tune every note on an instrument to simple fractions and call it a day?

The Pythagoreans actually tried: stack the best-sounding interval, the perfect fifth (multiply by 1.5), again and again, and whenever it goes past the octave, divide by 2 to fold it back. Ancient China's method of "adding and subtracting a third" (三分损益) followed the same idea. Try stacking them yourself and see what happens:

🥞 Starting from A 440, stack perfect fifths

After 12 stacks, you've visited all 12 note names and should be back at A. Instead you end up noticeably higher than the real A. The reason is simple: 1.5 to the 12th power is about 129.75, while 7 octaves is 2 to the 7th power, which is 128. Powers of 3 and powers of 2 can never be equal, so the books never balance. That little leftover is called the Pythagorean comma.

📜 Word archaeology: comma

In music, the leftover is called a comma, the same word as the punctuation mark. Greek komma, a little piece cut off. A comma is a small piece cut out of a sentence; a musical comma is a small piece left over from the octave.

The consequences were very real. Keyboard notes are fixed, so if you tuned one in C to sound perfectly pure, switching to another key meant hitting a horribly sour fifth. People called it the wolf fifth, because it howled like a wolf. Over the centuries, dozens of patch-up tuning systems were invented, each one robbing Peter to pay Paul.

The final solution: nobody gets to be pure, and everyone shares the error

Cut one octave (×2) into 12 equal steps, where each step up multiplies by the same number. Twelve steps have to double the frequency exactly, so that number multiplied by itself 12 times must equal 2:

f🎯 the frequency of the note you want = 440📌 starting point: A4, your open A string × 2n12🪜 climb n semitones. Each step multiplies by about 1.0595; twelve steps multiply by exactly 2

To go down, make n negative. For example, your open E string is 7 semitones above A: 440 × 2 to the 7/12 power, about 659.26 Hz. A pure fifth would be 440 × 1.5 = 660. The 0.74 Hz difference is your share of the error.

Why exactly 12 steps? Because 2 to the 7/12 power is about 1.4983, touchingly close to the pure fifth's 1.5. People have also experimented with 5, 7, 19, 31 and 53 steps, but 12 is the best value for money across three things: enough notes, accurate enough fifths, and a keyboard you can actually build.

🔤 Alphabet soup

12-TET: Twelve-Tone Equal Temperament. 12-EDO: Equal Division of the Octave, another name for the same thing.

Temperament: Latin temperare, to mix, to blend evenly. A relative of temper and tempera (egg paint). The idea is to water down the pure intervals and blend them evenly.

Semitone: semi means half; one of the 12 steps. Two steps make a whole tone. Cent: Latin centum, one hundred. Cut a semitone into 100 smaller pieces, and an octave is 1200 cents. The Englishman Alexander Ellis proposed it in 1885 specifically to measure these tiny differences. Most people start noticing something is off at around 5 to 10 cents; string players are more sensitive.

The shared bill: who loses the most?

Fifths and fourths barely lose anything, just 2 cents. Thirds and sixths take the biggest hit, off by more than ten cents. Check with your ears:

The pure major third is as calm as a lake. The equal-tempered one trembles slightly, more than ten times a second. Every piano in the world trembles like this, and after more than a century of listening, we've all gotten used to it.

🕰️ History gossip

Zhu Zaiyu (朱载堉), heir to the Prince of Zheng in Ming-dynasty China, published an exact method for twelve-tone equal temperament around 1584. Using an extra-large abacus with 81 rods, he calculated the 12th root of 2 to 24 decimal places. He later petitioned the emperor seven times to give his inherited title to someone else so he could focus on research: a scientist held back by nobility.

At almost the same time, the Dutch engineer Simon Stevin (the man who popularized decimal fractions) worked it out independently, but his manuscript wasn't published then. Neither copied the other.

Bach's The Well-Tempered Clavier is a famous case of mistranslation in music history. The original title, Das Wohltemperirte Clavier, means a keyboard tuned well. Bach most likely used a tuning that worked in every key but gave each key its own personality, not strict equal division. True equal temperament only took over in the 19th century, along with the industrial production of pianos.

🎻 Translation for violinists

Your fingerboard has no frets, so you've been secretly playing in just intonation by ear all along: when you play a major third, you instinctively place it a hair lower so it doesn't tremble. That's a gift, not bad intonation.

The price is that when you play with a piano, you have to adjust to it, because it can't move. Also, your four open strings are tuned in pure fifths, each 2 cents wider than the piano's. Counting down from A to G, that adds up to about 4 cents lower than the piano's G. When your teacher asks you to tune your G string up just a little, this chapter is why.

Decoding the alphabet soup: who named all these things?

We have our 12 steps. Next, they need official names. Every rule here is a historical leftover, not one of them was designed. So don't look for logic; look for gossip.

🎹 Why 7 white keys and 5 black keys?

Twelve notes is a lot, so people usually pick 7 of them as the main cast. That's a scale. The most common pick is the major scale, with step sizes of: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half.

The piano was designed by making the 7 notes of C major the white keys and squeezing the other 5 into the gaps as black keys. There's no black key between E and F or between B and C, because those pairs are only a semitone apart already: there's no gap to squeeze one into.

📜 Word archaeology

Scale: Latin scala, ladder. Milan's La Scala opera house is literally the Ladder Opera House.

Diatonic: Greek dia (through) plus tonos (tone), roughly walking through on whole steps. Chromatic: Greek chroma, color. Using all 12 notes is like giving the scale color. The Chrome browser shares the same root.

🔠 Letter names: C D E F G A B

Naming notes with letters is a medieval habit. In the theory system of the time, the lowest note was labeled A, then B, C, D going up. Centuries later, the major scale became the star, and the major scale with no sharps or flats happens to start on C. So here we are: the alphabet starts at A, but music lessons start at C. A got demoted to sixth place simply because times changed.

The number after the letter is the octave number: C4 is the C in the very middle of the piano (middle C), and A4 is 440, your open A string. The number goes up by one at every C, so the note right above B3 is C4.

🎤 Solfège: do re mi fa sol la si

Around the year 1000, an Italian monk named Guido, fed up with his choir's memory, noticed that in a hymn to Saint John, each line started one step higher than the line before. So he took the first syllable of each line as the note's name: Ut queant laxis, Resonare fibris, Mira gestorum, Famuli tuorum, Solve polluti, Labii reatum.

  • Ut became Do: in the 17th century, the Italian Giovanni Battista Doni found Ut hard to sing and swapped it for Do. Some say it comes from Dominus (Lord); others say it's the first two letters of his own surname. He has never been cleared of the self-promotion charge. The French still use Ut today.
  • Si: the initials of Sancte Iohannes (Saint John) in the hymn's last line, added later as the seventh syllable.
  • Si became Ti: in the 19th century, the English music teacher Sarah Glover changed Si to Ti so that every syllable would start with a different letter (Sol already had S). That's why The Sound of Music sings Ti.

♯ ♭ ♮: all three symbols came from the letter b

In medieval music, the note B came in a higher and a lower version. The lower one was written as a round-bellied b, the soft b; the higher one as an angular square b, the hard b.

  • The round b gradually turned into ♭ (flat: down a semitone).
  • The square b turned into ♮ (natural) and ♯ (sharp: up a semitone).
  • The square b looked so much like an h that German printers just printed H. So in Germany, H is our B, and B is our B flat. That's how Bach could write a melody from his own name: B, A, C, H.
  • The German words for major and minor, Dur and Moll, also come from hard b (durum) and soft b (molle).

Every black key has two names: a semitone above C is C♯, a semitone below D is D♭, and on the piano they're the same key.

📏 Intervals: the distance between two notes, and that fencepost count

An interval's number counts how many letter names it spans, including both ends. C to E: count C, D, E, that's three, so it's a third. C to G is five, a fifth. This is the fencepost counting we held a grudge against earlier, and it causes music's most famous math accident: a third plus a third equals a fifth (C to E, then E to G: the E gets counted twice).

The number alone isn't precise enough, because spanning the same three letter names can mean different sizes: C to E is 4 semitones, but D to F is only 3. So it needs a qualifier:

🔤 Alphabet soup: P, M, m, A, d, TT

P: Perfect. Reserved for unisons, fourths, fifths and octaves. These are the simple ratios Pythagoras personally approved, so they come in only one official size, with no major or minor.

M and m: Major and minor, Latin for larger and smaller. Seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths each come in two sizes, a semitone apart. Capital M is major, lowercase m is minor. This capitalization rule will show up constantly in chord names later.

A and d: Augmented (Latin augere, to enlarge) and diminished (Latin deminuere, to make smaller). One semitone more than perfect or major is augmented; one semitone less than perfect or minor is diminished.

TT: Tritone. Exactly 3 whole tones, 6 semitones, splitting the octave right down the middle. Medieval writers nicknamed it the devil in music.

Triads: skip one, take one

The most basic chords have three notes and are called triads. Building one is as simple as a buffet: pick a note on the scale as the foundation, then skip one, take one. Start on C, skip D, take E, skip F, take G. You get C, E, G.

Each of the three parts has a job title:

  • Root: the foundation. It decides the chord's name.
  • Third: a third above the root. It decides the chord's personality, cheerful or melancholy.
  • Fifth: a fifth above the root. It thickens the sound and doesn't have much of a temper of its own.

Remember that G string? The three overtones in the ratio 4:5:6 are exactly the root, third and fifth. With three keys, you're rebuilding the inside of a string.

Four kinds of triads: two building blocks, stacked four ways

Skip one, take one, and every two neighboring notes you take are a third apart. Thirds come in two sizes: a major third is 4 semitones, a minor third is 3. Stack two blocks in two layers, and there are four ways to do it:

Major and minor triads use exactly the same blocks, just with the layers swapped. The major triad's frequency ratio is 4:5:6, straight from the overtone series, which is why it sounds grounded. The minor triad is 10:12:15, with bigger numbers, so the waves line up less often and an indescribable shadow creeps in. The entire difference between major and minor is that middle note, the third, moving by one semitone.

🔤 Alphabet soup and word origins

Triad: Greek trias, a group of three. Related to trio and triple.

Major, minor: refers to whether the third above the root is the large size or the small size. It has nothing to do with volume, pitch or importance. In chord symbols, a bare capital letter means a major triad by default, and a minor triad adds a lowercase m.

dim: diminished. The fifth is shaved down a semitone. Also written as a small circle °. aug: augmented. The fifth is stretched up a semitone. Also written as a plus sign +.

sus: suspended. The third has been hung up and replaced by a second (sus2) or a fourth (sus4). With no third, there's no major or minor, so it sounds suspended in midair, waiting to come back down.

C5: just the root and fifth, not even a third. Rock guitarists call it a power chord. Strictly speaking, two notes don't make a chord, but nobody dares argue with a guitarist who has the distortion on.

Seventh chords: making sense of the alphabet soup

A triad is 1, 3, 5. Keep skipping one and taking one, stack another layer, and you get 1, 3, 5, 7: a seventh chord, named for the new note a seventh above the root. With four notes sounding together, the flavor suddenly goes from plain water to a layered soup.

Those chord names on sheet music that look like garbled text are actually a set of assembly rules. Just read them left to right. Let's dissect the scariest one:

C🏠 The root. The foundation is C
m🌧️ The third is a minor third. Without the m, it defaults to major
7🪜 Stack a seventh on top. A bare 7 means a minor seventh by default
♭5🔧 A modification: lower the fifth by a semitone

🗝️ The decoding rules, all of them

  1. The capital letter is the root. A letter on its own means a major triad.
  2. m, min, minus sign −: lower the third, making it minor.
  3. The number 7: add a seventh, and by default it's a minor seventh (10 semitones above the root). This is the biggest trap in the whole system.
  4. maj7, M7, triangle Δ: use a major seventh instead (11 semitones). Note that maj modifies the 7, not the chord itself. So CmM7 means a minor triad with a major seventh.
  5. dim, °: minor thirds stacked all the way up. ø (a circle with a slash): half-diminished, that is, m7♭5, only diminished halfway. aug, +: raise the fifth a semitone.
  6. sus2, sus4: the third gets replaced. add9: keep the triad as is and add a ninth. 6: add a major sixth.
  7. 9, 11, 13: keep skipping one and taking one on top. After 7, counting to 9 is really the 2 an octave higher, 11 is the 4 an octave higher, and 13 is the 6 an octave higher. That fencepost counting again.
  8. Slash: C/E reads "C over E," meaning a C chord with E as the lowest note. More on that in the next chapter.
🕰️ Why does a bare 7 default to a minor seventh?

Because the first seventh chord to become popular, and the one used most, is the one built on the fifth note of a major scale. In C major, that's G, B, D, F, and G up to F is exactly a minor seventh. It showed up so often that it claimed the shortest name, G7. The major seventh chord that came later had to make do with a few extra letters.

Its full name is the dominant seventh chord. We'll explain dominant two chapters from now. Fun fact: 4:5:6:7 in the overtone series is very close to a dominant seventh chord. That 7 is the overtone the piano doesn't have, from two chapters ago.

The same chord, many ways to arrange it

C, E and G don't have to have C at the bottom. As long as the members are these three, it doesn't matter who stands at the bottom, who stands on top, how far apart they are, or whether they sound together or take turns: it's still a C chord. A chord is defined by its members, not its formation.

🔄 Inversions: someone else stands at the bottom

Move the lowest note to the top and you've inverted the chord once. Each version tastes slightly different: root position is the most stable, first inversion feels lighter, and second inversion feels like it's hanging and wants to step down. Try turning on 📌 Sticky up top and arranging it yourself. The note under the keyboard will tell you what you've built.

This is what the slash from the previous chapter is for: C/E is first inversion, C/G is second inversion. Whatever comes after the slash is the lowest note.

📐 Voicing: standing close together or spreading out

This is called voicing: arranging each note as if it were a separate voice. The same note can appear several times without changing the chord's identity. When dozens of orchestra players sound a C major chord together, it's still just these three note names.

🤹 Broken chords: not sounding together still counts

Play the notes of a chord one at a time and your ears automatically collect them and imagine the complete chord. This is the most counterintuitive thing about chords: a chord can exist across time, not just in a single instant.

📜 Word archaeology

Arpeggio: Italian arpeggiare, to play like a harp (arpa). The Chinese term, 琶音, borrows its first character from the pipa, a Chinese lute.

Inversion: Latin invertere, to turn over.

Alberti bass: named after the 18th-century Venetian composer Domenico Alberti. His own music is hardly ever performed now, but his name is forever tied to do-sol-mi-sol. That's one way to become immortal.

🎻 Translation for violinists: you're the expert at broken chords

Because the bridge is curved, the bow can only press two strings firmly at once. So the three- and four-note chords on a violin are actually rolled: first the lower two strings, then the upper two. Every chord you play has a bit of arpeggio built in.

Bach's solo violin works take this trick to the limit: a single line throughout, but it jumps back and forth between registers, and listeners hear a whole building of harmony in their heads. Theory books call it implied harmony. A one-string player can perform the whole drama of chords alone.

Chord progressions: chords need a storyline too

A single chord is just a color. When several chords walk in a line, you get a story. That line is called a chord progression.

🏘️ The seven households of C major

Using only the 7 white keys of C major, skip one and take one starting on each note, and you get 7 triads. They're the native residents of this key, called diatonic chords:

Look closely: the same recipe produces major, minor and even diminished chords. That's because the white keys aren't evenly spaced (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half), so skipping one and taking one naturally lands on thirds of different sizes.

🔤 Alphabet soup: those Roman numerals

A Roman numeral says which note of the scale the chord is built on. Uppercase means a major triad, lowercase means minor, and a small circle means diminished. So I, IV and V are major, ii, iii and vi are minor, and vii° is diminished.

Why not just write C, F, G? Because if you change key, all the letters change, but the relationships don't. In C major, I, IV and V are C, F and G; in G major they're G, C and D, and it sounds like the same story. It's just like shifting position on your violin: your hand moves, but the relationships between your fingerings stay the same.

🧲 Three roles: home, going out, wanting to go home

  • Tonic (T, represented by I): home. The most restful place to stop. Same root as tone: the note that sets the key.
  • Dominant (D, represented by V): desperately wants to go home. Latin dominari, to rule. After the tonic, it's the note with the most say, and the moment it appears, it pulls the music toward home.
  • Subdominant (S, represented by IV): going out for a stroll. Sub here means below: the dominant is a fifth above the tonic, and the subdominant is a fifth below it. It doesn't mean a second-rate dominant.

Between the B and F in G7 is exactly a tritone, the devil's interval. It's extremely unstable: B wants to climb a semitone to C, F wants to fall a semitone to E, and when they let go, they land right inside a C chord. Several centuries of Western music boil down to playing this game of tension and release over and over.

💡 Listening cheat sheet

Major: stable, bright. Minor: stable, dark. Diminished: twisted, wants to escape. Augmented: floating, doesn't know where it's going. First decide stable or unstable, then bright or dark, and one four-way choice becomes two two-way choices.

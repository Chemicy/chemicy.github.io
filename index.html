Web pages that teach one idea at a time, by letting you take it apart with your own hands.

Each of these started with a question. Usually a friend was stuck on something I had already struggled through; sometimes the question was my own. Instead of explaining it in a message, I built a small page where the idea can be poked, tested and broken.

I have made more than thirty of them over the past year. The four below are my favorites, and they come from four fields that have nothing to do with each other. That is on purpose: the method matters more than the subject.

How they are made

I build these with an AI model as my partner. The division of work is clear, and it is the part I care most about getting right.

What I decide

  • Which idea is worth a page, and who it is for.
  • The way in: Venice for accounting, redstone for logic, a single string for harmony, a plain sentence for a theorem.
  • The order things are revealed, and what the reader gets to touch at each step.
  • What to cut. Most first drafts explain too much.
  • Whether it works, by watching a real person use it.

What the AI helps with

  • Turning my design decisions into working code, and fixing it when it breaks.
  • Drafting and translating text, which I then rewrite until it sounds right.
  • Gathering historical and technical background, which I check before keeping.

Why I make them

What I enjoy most is the moment an idea stops being a rule and starts making sense. It rarely comes from a better definition. It comes from the right way in, like a merchant's ledger, a redstone circuit or a single vibrating string, and from feedback that arrives while you are still thinking.

I grew up in a school system that rewarded the right answer on the test, where asking why a rule exists counted as a detour. Many of the things I understand best, I learned on those detours: by building things, by explaining them to friends, and in long conversations with AI models that were patient with every question I had. These pages are my attempt to pass that patience on. Every switch answers the moment you flip it, every quiz explains itself, and the detour is often the best way in.

Where this is going

Education has been the thread of my whole life, often as the thing I was fighting against, now as the thing I want to build.

I do not have a well-polished five-year plan. I have a direction. I know that there are classrooms, right now, where a student is being told she is behind, and the only feedback she will get is a number on a test she has already stopped caring about. I know that it does not have to work that way, because I have lived the alternative: instant feedback, the freedom to explore a detour, an AI partner who never runs out of patience.

A master's degree is my next step toward building that alternative at a scale larger than one page at a time. After it, I will carry what I have learned into China's vocational colleges, where I already have a seat at the table and where "learning by doing" is not a slogan but a founding promise that has yet to be fully kept.

I cannot tell you exactly what I will build. I can tell you that I will keep building.
